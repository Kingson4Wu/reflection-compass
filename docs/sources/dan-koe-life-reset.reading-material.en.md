## Introduction: Why New Year's resolutions often do not work

If you are anything like me, you probably think New Year's resolutions are stupid.

That is because the way most people try to change their lives is fundamentally wrong.

People make these resolutions simply because everyone else does. We attach a shallow sense of meaning to our behavior in a game of status competition. But these resolutions do not meet the conditions required for real change. Real change is far more complex than persuading yourself to be more disciplined and productive this year.

If this describes you, I am not trying to talk down to you. (I can be a little harsh in my writing.) I have abandoned more than ten times as many goals as I have achieved. I think that should be true of most people.

But one thing remains true: people try to change their lives and fail almost every time.

Although I think New Year's resolutions are stupid, reflecting on a life you dislike is still wise. Only then can you start moving in a much better direction.

Whether you want to start a business, change your body, or take a risk to pursue a more meaningful life without giving up two weeks later, I want to share seven ideas about behavior change, psychology, and productivity. You may not have encountered them before, and I hope they help you achieve your goals.

This will be a detailed article. It is not something to read and forget. Save it, take notes, and set aside time to think carefully.

The complete method at the end is designed to explore your inner world and uncover what you truly want from life. It takes about a full day to complete, but its effects may last much longer.

Let's begin.

## I. You do not have the life you want because you are not yet the person who can live it

When people set ambitious goals, they often focus on only one of the two conditions for success:

1. Change your actions to move toward your goal. This is the secondary, second-order factor.
2. Change who you are so that your behavior changes naturally. This is the primary, first-order factor.

Most people set a superficial goal, pump themselves up, and try to stay disciplined for a few weeks, then slip effortlessly back into their old way of life.

They are trying to build a beautiful life on a foundation that has already rotted.

If that feels too abstract, consider a few examples.

Picture a successful person: a bodybuilder with an impressive physique, a founder or CEO worth hundreds of millions of dollars, or a highly charismatic person who can talk to a group of strangers without anxiety.

Do you think the bodybuilder has to struggle to restrain themselves to eat healthily? Does the CEO have to force themselves to be disciplined enough to show up on time and lead their team?

On the surface, you might think so. In reality, they cannot imagine living any other way.

For the bodybuilder, eating unhealthily would be what required gritting their teeth. For the CEO, staying in bed after the alarm would be what required forcing themselves. They would hate every second of it.

(Of course there are complications here, but accept the example for now.)

To some people, my lifestyle may seem extreme or excessively disciplined. To me, it all feels natural.

I am not using this to belittle other lifestyles. I simply enjoy living this way.

When my mother tells me to take a break, go out, and have some fun, I hold back from saying:

“If what I do does not make me happy, why would I do it?”

The next sentence may sound simple, yet it is surprising how many people fail to understand it:

If you want a particular result in life, you must live the life that produces it before you actually obtain it.

If someone tells me they want to lose 30 pounds, about 13.6 kilograms, I often do not fully believe them.

Not because I think they cannot do it, but because that same person will too often say:

“I wish I could finish losing weight quickly so I could enjoy life again.”

Unfortunately, you need to understand this: if you are unwilling to adopt the lifestyle that helps you lose weight for the rest of your life, and have not found a new reason compelling enough to pull you away from old habits, you will eventually return to where you started.

Then you will have to admit, regretfully, that you wasted a resource you can never recover: time.

When you truly change yourself, habits that do not move you toward your goals become repulsive. You have developed a deep awareness of the life those behaviors will create as they accumulate.

You tolerate your current standards of living because you have not fully recognized what they are or where they will ultimately take you.

We will discuss how to discover this later, but first we need more groundwork.

You say you want to change. You say you want “financial freedom” and “a healthy body.” But your actions tell a different story.

There is a reason, and it runs deeper than you think.

## II. You do not have the life you want because you do not actually want to go there

> Trust only actions. Life happens in actual behavior and events, not words. Trust actions.
>
> — Alfred Adler

If you want to change yourself, you must understand how the mind works so that you can begin reshaping it.

The first step in understanding the mind is recognizing that all behavior is directed toward a goal.

In other words, we can understand human behavior through teleology.

On reflection, this seems obvious. But when we examine it deeply, most people are reluctant to accept it.

You take a step forward because you want to reach somewhere. You scratch your nose because you want to relieve an itch.

These examples are straightforward, but most of the time the goals you pursue are unconscious.

You may not realize, for example, that sitting on the couch doing nothing in broad daylight is a way of passing time to avoid your next responsibility.

More deeply and complexly, you may be pursuing goals that harm you while justifying your behavior with socially acceptable reasons so that you do not look like a failure.

For example, if you cannot stop procrastinating on your work, you may explain it by saying, “I lack discipline.”

But you are still pursuing a goal. That goal may be to protect yourself from others' judgment and criticism after you finish and publicly share your work.

Or you say you want to leave a dead-end job, yet stay there without any genuinely reasonable explanation. You might think you lack courage or are simply not someone who takes risks.

In fact, you may be pursuing security, predictability, and an excuse.

To the people around you, a stable job, even a dead-end one, may count as success. You do not want them to see you as a failure.

The lesson is this: real change requires changing the goals you pursue.

I do not mean setting another superficial goal. Goal setting itself may satisfy an unconscious need that is harming you. Productivity advice has covered that territory enough.

What I really mean is changing how you see the world.

A goal is itself a way of seeing the world. It is a projection into the future that becomes a lens through which you perceive reality, helping you notice information, ideas, and resources that support its achievement.

Next, we need to go deeper.

## III. You do not have the life you want because you are afraid to actually live it

> Remember that how an idea arose or where it came from does not matter at all.
>
> You may never have met a professional hypnotist or undergone formal hypnosis. But if you accept an idea—whether it comes from yourself, a teacher, a parent, a friend, an advertisement, or any other source—and firmly believe it to be true, its effect on you can be as powerful as a hypnotist's words on a hypnotized person.
>
> — Maxwell Maltz

There is a mechanism behind who you are today and how you will become someone else tomorrow.

This is how identity forms:

1. You want to achieve a goal.
2. You perceive reality through the perspective that goal provides.
3. You notice only the “important” information and ideas that help achieve it. This is learning.
4. You act and use feedback to confirm that you are moving toward your goal.
5. You repeat these behaviors until they become automatic and unconscious. This is conditioning.
6. These behaviors gradually become your definition of yourself: “I am someone who…”
7. To maintain psychological consistency, you begin defending your identity.
8. Your identity shapes new goals, and the cycle begins again.

If this identity does not support a good life, the consequences quickly become serious.

Unfortunately, you must break the cycle between steps six and seven.

But this process began when you were a child. Your most basic goal then was survival. You depended on your parents to teach you how to survive, so you had to learn to comply.

Most people teach through rewards and punishments. If you do not accept their beliefs and values, you are punished. Until you see through this mechanism, genuinely independent thought is difficult.

Your parents went through the same process, possibly throughout their lives. That is where the problem becomes dangerous.

Unless they actively broke this pattern, they may have been influenced by the industrial era's prevailing ideas of success. They also inherited the best and worst conditioning of their parents and grandparents.

Go a level deeper.

Once your physical survival needs are met—which is relatively easy in today's world, where many people live in safety almost from birth—you begin seeking survival at the level of ideas and ideology.

You may no longer need to protect and perpetuate your physical body, but you still fiercely protect and reproduce your thoughts.

The wars of ideas on the internet are an obvious example. They involve both individual and group identities.

When your body feels threatened, you enter a fight-or-flight response. Something similar happens when your identity is threatened.

If you strongly identify with a political ideology, you feel threatened when someone questions your beliefs. You may experience real physiological stress, emotionally as though someone has slapped you in the face.

Because most people do not carefully examine whether their emotions reflect reality, they can easily become trapped in echo chambers, growing more committed to views that harm themselves and others.

If you grew up in a religious family without thinking independently, you may fight back or attack when someone threatens the belief system on which your psychological security depends.

The same happens if you unconsciously define yourself as a particular kind of person: a lawyer, a gamer, or someone who “just does not take action to improve their life.”

## IV. The life you want exists at a particular stage of mental development

Over time, the mind moves through recognizable stages.

At birth, you are like a sponge absorbing survival experiences and the beliefs around you—largely determined by your culture—to gain a sense of safety.

Without attention, your mind can gradually become rigid, eventually making a meaningful life difficult.

Many theoretical models discuss this phenomenon, including Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Graves's stages of self-development, Spiral Dynamics, and Integral Theory. Even without studying these theories, similar patterns are easy to observe in society.

I have discussed these models many times and incorporated them into my Human 3.0 model. I have also designed AI prompts to help people explore their developmental stage and how they might grow next.

Here is a brief review of nine stages in the theory of ego development.

### 1. Impulsive

Cannot distinguish impulse from action and tends to think in black and white. A toddler may hit someone when angry because a clear boundary between emotion and behavior has not yet formed.

### 2. Self-Protective

Sees the world as dangerous and learns to protect themselves. A child learns to hide a report card, lie about completed chores, or guess what adults want to hear.

### 3. Conformist

Identifies the self with the group and treats the group's rules as reality itself. For example, someone cannot understand why anyone would vote differently from their family or group.

### 4. Self-Aware

Begins noticing a difference between inner experience and outward presentation. For example, someone in church suddenly realizes they may not actually believe what everyone around them believes, but does not know how to handle that feeling.

### 5. Conscientious

Develops a personal system of principles and holds themselves accountable to it. Someone may leave their family's religion after careful study and develop a personal philosophy they can defend, or create a career plan with clear milestones.

### 6. Individualist

Recognizes that their principles are shaped by context and stops treating them as absolute truths. They may discover how strongly their political views reflect their upbringing, or realize that their pursuit of career success is really a search for their father's approval.

### 7. Strategist

Can act within systems while recognizing that they are part of those systems. They may lead an organization while actively examining their blind spots, or participate in political discussion knowing their views are necessarily limited by biases and perspective.

### 8. Construct-Aware

Recognizes all conceptual frameworks, including personal identity, as useful constructions rather than absolute truths. They may treat spiritual beliefs as metaphors rather than literal facts, understand that the map is not the territory, and regard their role as a “founder” or “thought leader” with calm and a little humor.

### 9. Unitive

The sense of separation between self and life gradually dissolves. Work, rest, and play seem to merge. There is no longer a self that must strive to become someone, only a direct response to what is happening now.

I suspect most readers are somewhere between stages four and eight. That is a wide range.

Those closer to stage eight may simply want to learn something new or find a harmless way to pass time.

Those closer to stage four may truly be looking for change. They feel their life should hold more possibilities, but cannot yet make sense of everything because so many factors are involved.

The good news is that your present stage is not the most important thing. Moving from one stage to the next usually follows some common pattern.

## V. Intelligence is the ability to get the life you want

> The only real measure of intelligence is whether you can get what you want in life.
>
> — Naval Ravikant

There is a formula for success.

One ingredient is agency: the ability to take initiative and make things happen.

Another is opportunity. Many people mistake opportunity for privilege because they overlook the other ingredients.

The final ingredient is intelligence.

With strong agency but little opportunity, you can act energetically toward a goal and still produce little. With opportunity and agency but insufficient intelligence, you cannot make full use of the opportunity.

We have already discussed agency.

As for opportunity, I cannot tell you where to move. But if you cannot see the abundance of opportunities in the digital world before you, I do not know what to say.

I want to focus on what intelligence means in the context of agency, opportunity, and this article.

For that, we need cybernetics.

The word comes from Greek and carries the meaning of steering or skillful navigation. It can also be understood as the art of getting what you want.

If Naval defines intelligence as getting the life you want, understanding cybernetics can help us do so faster.

Cybernetics reveals the characteristics of intelligent systems:

- Set a goal.
- Act toward it.
- Sense your current position.
- Compare that position with the goal.
- Act again based on feedback.

You can judge a system's intelligence by its ability to iterate and keep advancing through trial and error.

A ship that drifts off course continually adjusts its direction toward its destination. A thermostat senses changes in temperature and decides whether to activate. When blood sugar rises, the pancreas secretes insulin.

What does this have to do with getting the life you want?

A great deal.

Acting, sensing your state, comparing, and understanding the system from a higher level are all core capacities of high intelligence.

Here, high intelligence means iterating, continuing to try, and understanding the whole picture. Low intelligence means failing to learn from mistakes.

People with low intelligence become trapped by problems instead of seeking solutions. They encounter an obstacle and give up.

For example, a writer fails to build an audience and quits because they cannot try new approaches, experiment, and find a process that works for them.

If you believe you can never build an effective process, that belief can be disproved by evidence, regardless of how many limiting beliefs you hold.

High intelligence means recognizing that, given enough time, many problems can be solved.

The reality is that you can achieve any goal you are determined to pursue.

Intelligence means recognizing that a sequence of choices can lead you toward your desired goal.

You understand that ideas and technologies have hierarchies: you cannot leap directly from papyrus to Google Docs.

Even if a goal cannot be achieved now, it may simply be because you lack necessary resources that could be created in the coming years.

When I discuss goals, I am not taking the usual self-help perspective, although that perspective can sometimes be useful.

I am talking about teleology and a view of the world as a whole: everything has a purpose, and everything is part of a larger whole.

Goals determine how you see the world. They determine how you define success and failure.

You can try to “enjoy the process,” but if you pursue the wrong goal, genuinely enjoying that process is difficult.

Your mind is the operating system through which you understand and experience reality, and that system is made of goals.

For most people, those goals were given to them by others. They resemble code written deep into your psyche:

Go to school. Get a job. Take offense at certain things. Play the victim. Retire at 65.

It is a familiar path through life, but it may not actually work.

To become more intelligent, you need to:

- Refuse to follow the familiar path blindly.
- Venture into the unknown.
- Set new, higher goals to expand your mind.
- Accept disorder and allow yourself to grow.
- Study universal principles in nature.
- Become someone with broad knowledge who can make connections across fields.

I know this may not be the conventional definition of intelligence.

But this sequence of steps helps your brain form many new connections, which ultimately make you act more intelligently.

Add agency, and you have a powerful combination.

That brings us to the next section.

## VI. How to begin a new life in one day

> The best periods of my life have always followed becoming completely fed up with making no progress.

How do you deeply understand your inner world? How do you recognize the past experiences and ideas that have shaped you? How do you gain insights profound enough to change your life's trajectory?

Through a simple but often painful act: questioning.

Very few people truly do this. Just listen to how people speak or observe their opinions on a topic, and you will see it.

Questioning is thinking, and few people truly think.

I want to give you a complete method you can use every year to reassess your life and begin a period of rapid growth.

It helps you ask the right questions, from the broadest level to the smallest:

Where do you want to go? What must you do to get there? What can you do now to move in that direction immediately?

The entire process takes a full day, so I recommend following it closely.

You need paper, a pen, and an open mind.

When I observe people who successfully change their identities, I find that they often experience a gradual accumulation of inner tension, then transform quickly at a particular moment.

More specifically, people usually move through three phases:

1. **Dissonance:** You feel you do not belong in your current life and become sufficiently tired of your stagnation.
2. **Uncertainty:** You do not know what to do next, so you either begin experimenting or lose your way and feel worse.
3. **Discovery:** You finally discover what you truly want to pursue and may make as much progress in six months as in the previous six years.

The method aims to help you reach the threshold of dissonance, move through uncertainty, and discover what you truly want to achieve.

That clarity should be strong enough that other things no longer distract you easily.

The process has three parts across the day:

- Morning: explore your psychology and discover hidden motives.
- Daytime: use scheduled reminders to interrupt autopilot and repeatedly reflect on your life.
- Evening: integrate the day's insights and decide your direction from tomorrow onward.

I cannot promise this will work for everyone, because I cannot guarantee that every reader is at a point in life where these ideas will resonate.

You cannot put a book's climax at the beginning and expect it to remain compelling.

### Part 1: Morning — Psychological excavation, vision, and anti-vision

First, you must build a new framework for your mind: a new perspective.

It is like leaving an old shell and beginning to enter a new one, gradually growing into the person who fits it.

At first, it may feel wrong for you. That is a good thing.

Take 15 to 30 minutes to think carefully and answer these questions. That is about the time it takes to watch a YouTube video. You can do this.

Do not outsource this thinking to AI.

You need to move beyond the limitations in your own thinking. If you cannot answer something now, return to it later.

#### First set: Discover dissatisfaction in your current life

1. What persistent dissatisfaction have you grown accustomed to tolerating? Not intense suffering, but things you have learned to accept. (If you do not dislike it, you will keep tolerating it.)
2. What have you repeatedly complained about over the past year without actually changing? Write down your three most frequent complaints.
3. For each complaint, if someone observed only your behavior and did not hear your words, what would they think you really wanted?
4. What truth about your present life would you be unwilling to tell someone you deeply respect?

These questions help you recognize what is painful in your current life.

Next, turn that pain into what I call an “anti-vision”: a clear understanding of the life you absolutely do not want.

You can then use those negative emotions to direct your effort positively and act from internal motivation.

#### Second set: Build your anti-vision

1. If nothing changes over the next five years, describe an ordinary Tuesday. Where do you wake up? How does your body feel? What is your first thought? Who is around you? What do you do from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m.? How do you feel at 10 p.m.?
2. Now extend that to ten years and answer again. What have you missed? Which opportunities have closed? Who has lost faith in you? What do people say about you when you are absent?
3. Imagine you have reached the end of your life, having always chosen the safest path and never broken old patterns. What was the cost? What did you never allow yourself to experience, attempt, or become?
4. Is anyone around you already living the future you just described? They may be five, ten, or twenty years ahead on that path. How do you feel when you imagine becoming like them?
5. What identity must you give up to truly change? For example, “I am someone who…” What social cost would you pay if you stopped being that person?
6. What is the most embarrassing real reason you have not changed? A reason that sounds weak, fearful, or lazy rather than rational and reasonable.
7. If your current behavior is protecting you, what exactly are you protecting? What is that protection costing you?

If you answered honestly, and you are at a suitable point in life for this reflection, you may feel deeply uneasy about your present life, even repulsed by it.

Next, we need to direct that energy positively.

We need a minimum viable vision, because a vision is like a product. Initially it may be vague, but time and experience will make it clearer and stronger.

#### Third set: Build your vision

1. Set practical conditions aside for a moment. If you could snap your fingers and live a completely different life three years from now, what would you truly want? Answer what you want, not what is realistic. What would an ordinary Tuesday look like? Describe it as specifically as before.
2. What must you believe about who you are for that life to feel natural rather than something you must force yourself to maintain? Write an identity statement: “I am someone who…”
3. If you were already that person, what is one thing you would do this week?

Answer these questions first thing after you wake up tomorrow.

### Part 2: Throughout the day — Interrupt autopilot and break unconscious patterns

These journaling exercises sound good, but what we actually want is change.

Frankly, that change will not happen unless you break the unconscious patterns keeping you where you are.

Throughout the day, keep thinking about everything you wrote in the morning. Beyond that, I do not want you to forget to reflect.

Take this seriously. You cannot truly change if you repeat the same things throughout your life. You must deliberately interrupt existing patterns.

Take out your phone now and set reminders or calendar events. Put the questions directly into them so you can start thinking as soon as they appear.

The more random the times, and the less they interfere with your usual schedule, the better.

#### Reflection reminders throughout the day

- **11:00:** What is the thing I am doing right now helping me avoid?
- **13:30:** If someone filmed my behavior over the past two hours, what would they think I wanted from life?
- **15:15:** Am I moving toward the life I dislike or the life I want?
- **17:00:** What deeply important thing am I pretending does not matter?
- **19:30:** Which of today's actions protected my identity rather than expressing what I actually wanted? (Hint: this may happen far more often than you think.)
- **21:00:** When today did I feel most alive? When did I feel most like the walking dead?

You can also reflect on these questions while commuting, walking, or lying down to rest:

- How would my life change if I no longer needed others to see me as a particular kind of person?
- Where in my life am I sacrificing vitality for security?
- What is the smallest version of my ideal self that I could become tomorrow?

### Part 3: Evening — Integrate insights and begin a period of sustained progress

If you took the earlier process seriously, I would be surprised if you had not gained at least one profound insight capable of changing your life's direction.

Now we need to make these insights explicit, integrate them into your identity, and act on them, gradually entering a new stage of mental development.

1. After today's reflection, what now seems the truest explanation for why you have remained stuck?
2. What is the real enemy? Name it clearly. Not outside circumstances or other people, but the internal pattern or belief that has been governing you.
3. In one sentence, describe what you absolutely refuse to let your life become. This is your condensed anti-vision. Reading it should produce a real emotional response.
4. In one sentence, describe the life you are working to build. Remember that your vision will keep evolving. This is your minimum viable vision.

Finally, we need to set goals.

Again, these goals are not about completing some achievement.

Goals are essentially projections into the future. They are unreliable and can make you feel bound to something that will change sooner or later.

Think of a goal instead as a perspective through which to observe the world: a lens that puts you in the right state of mind to act and move away from the life you do not want.

Do not worry excessively about the finish line, because you will eventually discover there is no finish line.

The real enjoyment comes from continuous progress.

Answer these questions:

1. **One-year perspective:** What specific change must exist a year from now to prove you have broken the old pattern? Write down one concrete thing.
2. **One-month perspective:** What must be completed a month from now to keep the one-year goal possible?
3. **Daily perspective:** What two or three actions would the person you are becoming naturally take tomorrow? Schedule them.

That is a lot of material. I hope it helps.

But we need one final step to bring it all together. Keep reading.

## VII. Turn your life into a video game

> The ideal state of inner experience is one in which consciousness has order.
>
> This order arises when psychic energy—attention—is invested in realistic goals, and a person's skills match the opportunities for action.
>
> Pursuing a goal brings order to consciousness because it requires focusing attention on the task at hand and temporarily forgetting everything else.
>
> — Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

You now have everything needed to build a good life.

Next, organize all your insights into a coherent plan.

Take a fresh sheet of paper and write down these six components:

1. **Anti-vision:** What is the greatest source of pain in my life? What kind of life do I never want to experience again?
2. **Vision:** What do I think my ideal life looks like? How can I improve that vision as I move forward?
3. **One-year goal:** What should life look like a year from now? Is it closer than today to the life I truly want?
4. **One-month project:** What must I learn? Which skills must I acquire? What can I build to move closer to the one-year goal?
5. **Daily levers:** Which tasks have the highest priority and actually advance progress, helping bring my project closer to completion?
6. **Constraints:** What am I unwilling to sacrifice to build my ideal life from the ground up? Why?

Why is this method so powerful?

Together, these six components create a small world of your own.

If you are at a suitable point in life to pursue this set of goals, you will find yourself becoming fully absorbed. You will feel drawn by a higher purpose and gradually find other options less important.

You have turned life into a video game.

Video games excel at creating three experiences: immersive engagement, enjoyment, and flow.

Games contain all the elements that help people focus and gain clarity. If we reverse-engineer those elements, we can apply them to life for deeper enjoyment, fewer distractions, and better results.

Specifically:

- Your **vision** is how to win the game, at least until the game itself changes.
- Your **anti-vision** is what you stand to lose. What happens if you fail or give up?
- Your **one-year goal** is the current main quest: the most important priority at this stage of life.
- Your **one-month project** is a boss battle. Through it, you gain experience points and unlock new abilities and resources.
- Your **daily levers** are the tasks to complete each day. These daily actions open new opportunities.
- Your **constraints** are the rules. They create limitations, but those limitations also stimulate creativity.

All these elements form concentric circles, like a force field protecting your mind from distractions and tempting new things.

The longer you play, the stronger the force field becomes.

Eventually, this way of life becomes part of your identity.

And you will no longer want another way of living.

— Dan

## Concluding editorial summary from the supplied material

If this article is compressed into a chain of reasoning, it runs roughly as follows:

1. **See the life you do not want clearly.** Anti-vision: recognize the long-term cost of maintaining the status quo.
2. **Clarify what you truly want.** Vision: establish new goals and a new perspective.
3. **Change your identity and behavior patterns.** Gradually become someone who naturally takes these actions, rather than merely forcing yourself to act.
4. **Build an action system with continuous feedback.** One-year goal → monthly projects → daily actions → feedback and adjustment.

In my view, the most notable part is not the title's promise to “change your life in one day,” but the more specific problem the author is trying to solve:

Why do people repeatedly make choices that oppose their goals even when they know what they should do?

His explanation is that people often try to change behavior without examining the goals those behaviors serve or why they need to maintain their present identity.

The method's value lies in turning vague dissatisfaction into clear direction, then turning that direction into daily actions that can be carried out and evaluated through feedback.

However, combining personal views with concepts such as psychological developmental stages and cybernetics does not mean every claim in the article has been scientifically established. For example, the claim that “given enough time, you can achieve any goal” is clearly an overgeneralization. External conditions, resource constraints, and luck still affect outcomes.

It is therefore more reasonable to understand this as a framework for self-reflection and action planning, rather than a scientific formula guaranteeing that your life will change in one day.
